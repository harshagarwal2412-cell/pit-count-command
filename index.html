Working prototype Case study · Civic Roundtable Aug 2026

The count is one night.
The coordination is eleven weeks.

A Continuum of Care running a HUD Point-in-Time count coordinates thirty-plus partner organizations toward a single January night, and the work that decides whether the count is any good happens in the eleven weeks before it. That work is currently done in a spreadsheet and an inbox. This is a prototype of what it looks like when an agent runs it instead.

Harsh Agarwal
01 / The read

Why coordination, not retrieval

Reading the product from the outside, the AI layer looks like it does retrieval well — verified answers pulled out of the files and workspaces a network has already accumulated. That is the right first move, and it is genuinely hard to do safely in government. It is also the move that everyone in the category is making.

The harder and more defensible thing sits one step further out. Retrieval answers a question someone thought to ask. Coordination is the problem of nobody asking the question in time — the partner org that goes quiet in week six, the rural zone that is short two teams, the domestic violence provider whose data has to be aggregated separately and was never confirmed. Those failures are invisible until they are expensive, and they are exactly the failures that live in the seams between Workspaces, Forms, Events, and the CRM. Nobody else in govtech is positioned to see across all four at once. You already are.

So I picked the sharpest instance of that problem I could find in a vertical Roundtable clearly leads with, and built the thing. The HUD Point-in-Time count is annual, federally mandated, deadline-locked to a single night between January 22 and 31, dependent on volunteer labor in the coldest week of the year, and executed across dozens of organizations that do not report to each other. It is a coordination problem with a scoreboard — and Roundtable's own blog is running a 2026 PIT debrief with Maricopa County and Prince William County, so whether it matters is not in question.

THE PRIMITIVES

You already have the inputs

Partner records in the CRM, submissions through Forms, count-night logistics in Events, methodology docs in the Knowledge Base. The data is there. Nothing reads across it on a clock.

THE GAP

Retrieval is passive

An agent that only answers when asked cannot catch the thing nobody knew to look for. Coordination failure is silent by definition.

THE WEDGE

A deadline makes it measurable

PIT has a fixed date, a federal spec, and a prior-year baseline. That means the agent's value is provable in one cycle, not argued about in a QBR.

03 / Before building it

How to know this worked

Success metrics set before the build and instrumentation in place at launch: here is what to commit to before a line of production code. The counterfactual is available and cheap: every CoC has last year's numbers.

What we're movingInstrumented asWhy this one
Coordinator hours per cycletime_in_coordination_surface, self-reported cycle logThe blunt one. If we don't give a coordinator back a working week, the agent is a novelty. Baseline from prior-cycle interview.
Late partner submissionssubmission_ts vs. deadline_ts, per org, per cycleDirect proxy for whether proactive nudges beat reactive chasing. Cleanest year-over-year comparison we have.
Zone coverage at T-14coverage_ratio snapshot, dailyCoverage fixed two weeks out is fixable. Coverage discovered on count night is not. Moving the discovery date earlier is the product.
HUD data-quality flags returnedhdx_flag_count, post-submissionThe outcome the CoC is actually graded on, and the one that survives a budget conversation.
Findings acted on vs. dismissedfinding_id → action taken / dismissed / ignoredThe honest trust metric. A high dismiss rate means the agent is noisy, and I'd rather find that in week two than in the renewal.

The last row is the one I'd watch hardest. An agent that surfaces twelve findings a coordinator ignores is worse than no agent — it trains people to close the tab. I'd ship with a deliberately high bar for what gets surfaced and loosen it only against that signal.

04 / Open questions

Five questions to answer first

Built from public information, which means the parts most likely to be wrong are the parts only the team inside can see.

01

Does a CoC coordinator actually live in Roundtable daily, or is the daily user the partner org and the coordinator a weekly visitor? That inverts who the agent should be talking to.

02

How much of a network's state is genuinely in the platform versus still in the coordinator's own spreadsheet? An agent reasoning over 60% of reality is worse than one that knows it has 60%.

03

Where has proactive AI already been tried and rejected here? The team has likely learned something about tolerance for an agent that speaks first, and that lesson is worth inheriting rather than rediscovering.

04

Is PIT the right first vertical, or is it seasonal enough that an always-on surface is a better wedge — election certification, or the continuous side of emergency response?

05

What does the deployments team say is the real reason a network stalls in month three? That answer probably reorders everything above.
